Quantifying Cryptographic Strength: Shannon Entropy & NIST 800-63B Defense

How we combined Claude Shannon's 1948 mathematical information theory with NIST password guidelines to detect hardcoded secrets and estimate brute-force resistance against 100 GH/s GPU clusters.

Quick Summary / Direct Answer: Heuristic password complexity rules (e.g. requiring symbols and digits) fail to measure real resistance against modern 100 GH/s GPU cracking rigs. By applying Shannon's information entropy $E = L \log_2(R)$ alongside NIST SP 800-63B pattern penalties (repetition, sequences, dictionary terms), the Cybersecurity Risk Analyzer computes an exact bit-metric of randomness and projects realistic offline brute-force attack durations.

1. The Mathematics of Information Entropy

Claude Shannon defined information entropy as the average rate at which information is produced by a stochastic source of data. For a string of length $L$ drawn from an alphabet of size $R$:

E = L · log₂(R)

In practical vulnerability analysis, we classify the character pool R dynamically:

  • Numeric only: R = 10 (log₂(10) ≈ 3.32 bits/char)
  • Alphanumeric lower: R = 36 (log₂(36) ≈ 5.17 bits/char)
  • Full mixed case + numbers: R = 62 (log₂(62) ≈ 5.95 bits/char)
  • Base64 / Symbols: R = 94 (log₂(94) ≈ 6.55 bits/char)

2. GPU Cluster Brute-Force Estimation

Modern distributed password-cracking rigs equipped with multiple NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs can compute approximately 100 Billion hashes per second (100 × 10⁹ hashes/sec for NTLM/MD5).

The expected time to recover a secret with total combination space S = R^L is given by:

T(seconds) = (R^L / 2) / (100 × 10⁹)

Under NIST 800-63B standards:

  • Critical (< 35 bits): Crackable in milliseconds to seconds.
  • Moderate (35 – 60 bits): Vulnerable within hours or days of distributed cracking.
  • Strong (> 60 bits): Computationally intractable (centuries to millennia).
